璇璣圖

The Star Gauge

璇璣圖 Xuánjī Tú, by Su Hui 蘇蕙, fourth century

A square of 841 characters, 29 to a side, that keeps turning into poems. Read it along the rim, around its inner squares, back and forth like a shuttle, or straight through the middle, and the same characters fall into new rhymed lines of three, four, five, six and seven syllables. Tradition says Su Hui wove it in five colours of silk for a husband who had left her.

Five threads

The colours are the reading instructions. Each one marks a set of blocks with its own line length, so a reader who can see the colours knows where a line starts and stops. The brocade itself is lost, and so is its original colouring. This page uses the division printed in the novel Flowers in the Mirror in the 1820s. Other reconstructions colour the same blocks differently: David Hinton’s shows the corners in blue.

Switch Colours off above the grid to see what many later readers saw. An older note, relayed by the Qing court editors, says that once copies stopped being coloured people could no longer find the line breaks. Kang Wanmin, the Ming scholar who counted the most poems, wrote every character in its own colour for that reason.

Frame 216 characters, lines of seven

Eight straight runs of characters: the rim, plus two rows and two columns inside it. They cross at 24 points, and the stretch from one crossing to the next is always a seven-character line that makes sense in both directions. At each crossing you choose where to turn. Four lines make a poem.

Corners 144 characters, phrases of three

Four blocks of six by six. Each row holds two three-character phrases, and the usual path runs back and forth: right to left along one row, left to right along the next.

Sides 312 characters, lines of four or six

Four blocks of six by thirteen: two halves with a six-character spine between them. Read one half at a time for lines of six. Take one character from the near half, one from the spine and the whole far half, and you have two lines of four. Each block keeps to a subject: the Book of Songs in the north, royal favourites in the east, time and decline in the south, the wife set aside in the west.

Inner arms 80 characters, lines of five

Four small blocks around the centre, four lines each. The two at the sides are read in columns, the two above and below in rows.

Inner squares and ring 80 characters, lines of four and five

Four squares of sixteen characters, and a ring of sixteen around the centre that describes the poem itself.

Signature and heart 9 characters

Eight characters that name the maker and the work, around 心, “heart”, at the exact centre. Colourings of this spot vary; here it takes the red of the frame.

Walk the frame

The frame is the part you compose yourself. Start on any crossing and choose a direction: that is a seven-character line. At the next crossing choose again. Four lines make a quatrain, and it will usually rhyme, because of where the rhyme words have been placed.

Press Start walking, then choose a ringed character on the grid.

    Counting each stretch in both directions, the frame has 64 lines. If you never turn straight back there are 352 four-line routes through it, and 1,456 if doubling back is allowed. Traditional readers also shifted the starting point by a character or skipped a line, which is how the totals climb: David Hinton counts 2,848 four-line poems in the frame alone.

    What makes it remarkable

    1. Every crossing rhymes

      The lines of the frame meet at 24 crossings, and the heart makes 25. In modern Mandarin every one of those characters ends in -in, -en or -un. Read any stretch of the frame so that it finishes on a crossing and it lands on that rhyme, whichever of the 64 lines you chose. This is why the backward quatrain still rhymes.

      In the Chinese of Su Hui’s time these were neighbouring sounds, some ending in -m or -ng, so the match was looser than it now looks.

    2. Each ring keeps its own rhyme

      Read clockwise, all sixteen lines of the rim end in -ang. The eight lines of the inner square end on a second rhyme, the group of 多 duō, 何 hé, 羅 luó, 華 huá and 沙 shā, which has drifted apart in Mandarin. The four lines around each corner block, read clockwise, end in -ang as well. So the sound tells you where on the frame you are.

    3. The corners are read like weaving

      A corner block is read the way a shuttle crosses a loom: along one row, down, and back along the next. The oldest accounts say the poem was woven, and that it was read by turning round and round.

    4. A borrowed spine

      Down the middle of each side block runs a spine of six characters that belongs to both halves. Run a spine on into the frame and it becomes one more seven-character line, ending on a rhyming crossing. In the north block the spine is a list of musical terms: 南 鄭 歌 商 流 徵, the southern airs, the songs of Zheng, song, the note shang, flowing, the note zhi.

    5. The diagonals work too

      The diagonal of each corner block, read into the inner corner of the frame, is another seven-character line that ends on the rhyme: 嗟中君容曜多欽 in the north-east, 廊桃燕水好傷身 in the north-west.

    6. It describes itself

      The ring around the centre is a chain of four lines, each starting on the character the last one ended on. Its final line says “a thread with no end and no beginning: the poem”, and its last character is its first.

    7. It is signed

      Inside the ring, eight characters give a place, a family name and a title: Lady Su of Shiping, turning-sphere diagram poem. The ninth square, the centre, is empty in the oldest count of 840 characters. Later copyists filled it with 心, heart.

    8. It carries a library

      Many phrases are borrowed from books every educated reader knew by heart, above all the Book of Songs, whose opening sections were read as poems about wives and husbands. Choose a phrase to see where it sits.

    9. Nobody agrees how many poems it holds

      • 200+the preface that circulated under Empress Wu Zetian’s name
      • 3,752the monk Qizong, under the Song or Yuan
      • 7,958Kang Wanmin, under the Ming, adding 4,206 of his own
      • 2,848David Hinton, 2008, for four-line poems in the frame alone
      • 10,000+totals reported in recent scholarship

      The Qing editors who copied Kang’s book into the imperial library were not impressed. Ask only that lines rhyme, they wrote, and the number can grow without end.

    Two texts, two rhyme systems

    English accounts describe one text of the Star Gauge. Chinese editors and museum curators also print a second, the one written on a handscroll in the National Palace Museum in Taipei, catalogued under Su Hui’s name and once said to carry a note by the Song poet Zhu Shuzhen dated 1230. Writing for the museum’s art magazine, its editors judge that note and the painter’s signature to be later additions, yet suggest the scroll’s wording may be the earlier one.

    The scroll has 840 characters and leaves the centre empty. Turned a quarter-turn so the frames line up, it shares only about a third of its cells with the familiar text. The frame is the same poem with variants; inside it, the corner poems have moved to other corners, the inner squares are turned and mirrored, and many lines are reworded.

    Which text is older? Ask the rhymes

    Rhyme categories in Chinese shifted between the third and the seventh centuries, and a rhyme dictionary of 1008, the Guangyun, records the old categories word by word. Turn on Rhymes and every line-end in the grid, 153 positions in all, takes the colour of its rhyme group. Point at one to see its exact rhyme, or turn on Pinyin as well to print the rhyme over every coloured character.

    • The south block keeps 支 apart. In the familiar text eleven of its twelve line-ends are 支-rhyme words. The scroll mixes in 脂 and 之: where the familiar text quotes the Book of Changes, 無日不陂 “no day without a slope”, the scroll has 悲 “sorrow”, and 差 becomes 私. Those swaps only rhyme after the three categories merged, as they had by the Tang.
    • 脂 rhymes with 微. The familiar text pairs them in three sets, the habit of Wei and Jin poets. The scroll’s version of the south-west corner poem is pure 微.
    • 真 runs with 文. Read backwards, the familiar south-west corner rhymes 文 and 真 words in one run, as early poets did; later poets kept them apart. Two of the scroll’s corner poems change rhyme every two lines instead.
    • Both texts rhyme -n with -m. At the crossings both put 侵 words, which ended in -m, among 真 words ending in -n. Standard practice of neither period explains that.

    Taken together, the familiar text rhymes the way verse of Su Hui’s own era did, and the scroll looks tidied toward later ears. That makes the familiar text the older of the two, and it is evidence, from inside the poem, for an early date.

    How far to trust this: each rhyme set has only six to sixteen words; a learned imitator could copy old rhymes; and where a word has several readings the most common one in its set was taken. The test was run for this page and has not been checked by a historical phonologist. I found no published study that does it.

    History

    Almost everything known about Su Hui was written down long after she lived, and the story changes with each telling. So does the size of the poem.

    1. 350s to 380s

      Su Hui, courtesy name Ruolan 若蘭, grows up in Shiping, in what is now Shaanxi, under the Former Qin, the northern state ruled by Fu Jian from 357 to 385. She marries Dou Tao 竇滔, who serves as prefect of Qinzhou. Nothing written in her lifetime survives.

    2. 400s to 500s

      Poets begin alluding to a wife’s brocade letter. A history of the northern states by Cui Hong, quoted in a later encyclopedia, says she wove a palindrome poem to redeem her husband’s offence. Around 500 the critic Liu Xie credits the palindrome form to someone else, which later editors took as a sign that the diagram was not yet in circulation.

    3. 648

      The Book of Jin gives her a short biography among its exemplary women. Dou Tao has been banished to the flowing sands. She weaves a palindrome poem in a revolving diagram and sends it to him. It is read turning round and round, its words are deeply sorrowful, and it runs to 840 characters, too many for the historian to copy out.

    4. 692, as dated

      A Record of the Woven Brocade Palindrome appears under the name of Empress Wu Zetian, and supplies nearly everything people now repeat. Dou Tao keeps a concubine, Zhao Yangtai, an unrivalled singer and dancer. Su Hui, quick-tempered and jealous, has her beaten. When Dou Tao is posted to Xiangyang he takes Zhao; Su Hui, aged twenty-one, refuses to go, and he stops writing. In remorse she weaves a brocade eight cun square, about eight inches, in five colours, holding more than two hundred poems, more than eight hundred words. People cannot read it. She says it makes its own language, and only the one she loves will understand. Dou Tao sends Zhao away and brings his wife home with full ceremony. This text also gives the piece the name it has kept.

      Scholars doubt Wu Zetian wrote it. Qiaomei Tang traces the jealous-wife story to the late Tang, a century or more after the empress, and the Qing court bibliographers wrote that they did not know where either the diagram or the preface came from.

    5. 900s

      A compilation made under Li Fang at the Song court preserves the earliest surviving excerpts of the 840-character text.

    6. 1100s to 1300s

      Sang Shichang prints the diagram in an anthology of reversible verse. A note attributed to a woman named Zhu Shuzhen tells how her father bought a copy and how she worked out its nested squares. A monk known as Qizong sorts the text by colour into seven charts and reads 3,752 poems out of them. Handwritten copies are credited to famous women of the Song, and several of those attributions are false. One copy, said to be in the hand of the painter Guan Daosheng (1262 to 1319), already cites Qizong, which is how he is dated.

    7. Ming dynasty

      Kang Wanmin, a scholar from Wugong, Su Hui’s own home county, adds an eighth chart and 4,206 more poems in his Reading Methods for the Xuanji Diagram Poems, for a total of 7,958. He names the five colours as vermilion, ink, blue-green, purple and yellow, and writes each character in its colour so that the true face of Lady Su’s loom can be seen at a glance. Handscrolls attributed to the painter Qiu Ying show her weaving the brocade and sending it off.

    8. 1781

      The editors of the imperial Siku library copy Kang’s book and add a cool note. If you ask only that lines rhyme and do not ask what they mean, you can go on finding more and more. That this was Su Hui’s intention, they write, we cannot believe, and they keep the book as a pastime for the garden of the arts.

    9. 1820s

      Li Ruzhen’s novel Flowers in the Mirror prints the whole grid in chapter 41 and has its characters work out reading rules colour by colour. Most modern Chinese editions, and the colours on this page, descend from it.

    10. Since 2007

      Eugene Wang reads the diagram against medieval star lore. David Hinton publishes an English version under the name Star Gauge in 2008. Michèle Métail opens her history of reversible poems with it, in French in 2011 and in English in 2017. Between 2016 and 2020 Jen Bervin and Charlotte Lagarde have it embroidered again in Suzhou, in double-sided silk.

    The name

    璇 xuán is fine jade and 璣 jī is something that turns. Old texts use the pair for the bowl stars of the Northern Dipper, for the pole star, or for an armillary sphere, the ringed instrument used to track the sky. English versions call the poem Star Gauge (Hinton), The Map of the Armillary Sphere (Métail) or Picture of the Turning Sphere (Wang). Hinton and Bervin both read the red frame as the rings of the instrument, with the heart at its still centre.
